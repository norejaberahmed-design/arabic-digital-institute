# Unit Blueprint 01 — From Zero to Your First Arabic Conversation

**Product:** Arabic Digital Institute (ADI)  
**Unit ID:** `PRE-A1-U01-FIRST-CONVERSATION`  
**Audience:** English-speaking adult absolute beginners; phone-first learners  
**Delivery:** Three short sessions, each approximately 8–12 minutes  
**Instruction language:** English with Arabic used progressively  
**Arabic variety:** Contemporary, broadly understandable Arabic; clear shared forms  
**Status:** Instructional blueprint for review; not an accredited CEFR/ACTFL course

## 1. Unit promise

By the end of this unit, a learner who starts with no Arabic can take part in a short, supported first-meeting exchange: greet someone, ask their name, give their own name, and close politely. The learner will also understand how Arabic text is oriented and recognize the first four target letters in controlled examples.

This unit does **not** promise full Arabic literacy, fluent conversation, or accurate phonetic scoring. The spoken exchange is initially learned as useful language chunks while script knowledge is developed in a separate, clearly signposted strand.

## 2. Measurable outcomes

At the end of the unit, the learner can:

1. Identify that Arabic is normally written from right to left.
2. Recognize ا، ب، ت، ث in isolated form and identify their basic visual differences.
3. Recognize that ب، ت، ث share a base shape and differ by dot placement; identify ا as a distinct form.
4. Understand the meaning and communicative purpose of:
   - مرحبا — Hello
   - أهلا — Hi / Welcome
   - ما اسمك؟ — What is your name?
   - اسمي ... — My name is ...
   - شكرا — Thank you
5. Complete a short first-meeting exchange with a model, then with reduced prompts.
6. Read the target greeting chunks by recognition after they have been explicitly introduced, without being told that every letter in those chunks has already been taught.
7. Produce a personally meaningful response by inserting their own name.

### Evidence by skill

| Skill | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Listening | Selects the correct meaning of a greeting and identifies the name question in a short recording |
| Speaking / interaction | Completes a 4-turn exchange; can use a text prompt if speech input is unavailable |
| Reading | Recognizes the taught letter set and matches familiar greeting chunks to meaning |
| Writing | Traces or selects the target letters; types or assembles اسمي followed by their own name |

## 3. Scope and guardrails

- Teach only five useful expressions in the first conversation. Do not overload the learner with grammar terminology.
- Keep English instructions concise and supportive. The learner may reveal more Arabic competence than assumed; offer a skip-ahead diagnostic later, not in this first experience.
- Do not teach a regional dialect as universal. Explain that مرحبا and أهلا are broadly understood; regional alternatives can appear in later labelled notes.
- Keep beginner display text mostly unvowelled. Use a vowel-marked form only in a clearly labelled sound-focus moment, then return to the normal unvowelled form.
- Transliteration is a temporary support, not a permanent parallel reading system. Offer a hide/show control and fade it in later review.
- Do not assess a learner's accent or exact articulation using browser speech-to-text. Transcript matching is not phonetic pronunciation scoring.
- Names may be typed in Latin script at this stage. This is a communication success, not a failure of Arabic writing.

## 4. Learning sequence

### Session 1 — Welcome to Arabic (8–10 minutes)

**Mission:** Enter the Arabic learning space and understand how Arabic writing works.

**Scene:** A calm virtual welcome room with an instructor avatar, a large Arabic word on a board, and a right-to-left directional animation. The current implementation may use a lightweight CSS scene or a 2D fallback; do not describe it as a full WebGL 3D environment.

**Flow**
1. Welcome: “You do not need to know any Arabic to begin.”
2. Show العربية and animate a reading pointer from right to left.
3. Ask the learner to choose the correct reading direction.
4. Introduce ا، ب، ت، ث one at a time.
5. Show the visual contrast: ب (one dot below), ت (two dots above), ث (three dots above), while ا has a different upright form.
6. Show the four letters in a simple recognition grid.
7. Learner completes a low-pressure matching activity.

**Vocabulary / script targets**
- ا — alif
- ب — baa
- ت — taa
- ث — thaa (the English “th” sound in “think” is an approximate guide, not an exact substitute)

**Practice**
- Tap the letter named by the instructor.
- Match each letter to its visual identity.
- Trace or select the correct letter on mobile.

**Feedback examples**
- Correct: “Yes. ب has one dot below the base shape.”
- Incorrect: “Look at the dots. ت has two dots above; ب has one below. Try again.”

**Exit check**
- Correctly identify the writing direction.
- Correctly identify at least 3 of the 4 letters, with feedback and retry available.

### Session 2 — Understand your first expressions (8–12 minutes)

**Mission:** Understand a greeting and the question “What is your name?”

**Scene:** A friendly first-meeting vignette. Two animated characters greet each other; speech bubbles appear with audio controls.

**Model exchange**
- A: مرحبا — Hello.
- B: أهلا — Hi / Welcome.
- A: ما اسمك؟ — What is your name?
- B: اسمي Alex. — My name is Alex.

Use a replaceable sample name and then invite the learner to insert their own name.

**Flow**
1. Listen to the complete exchange once without interruption.
2. Replay each line with English meaning.
3. Tap مرحبا or أهلا when hearing “Hello / Hi”.
4. Reveal the question chunk ما اسمك؟ and its meaning.
5. Reveal the answer frame اسمي ... and invite the learner to complete it.
6. Introduce شكرا as a polite closing / thank-you expression.
7. Practice meaning before asking for production.

**Micro-pattern discovery**
- اسمي means “my name” in this useful expression.
- ما اسمك؟ is learned as a complete question chunk at this stage.
- Avoid a full possessive-pronoun or grammatical case explanation.
- Explain that Arabic may use different spoken endings depending on whom one addresses; this beginner unit teaches the written form as a useful shared model and postpones detailed gender/register contrasts.

**Pronunciation support**
- مرحبا — mar-ha-ban (simple learner guide)
- أهلا — ah-lan
- ما اسمك؟ — ma is-muk? (one common masculine-address reading; explain that spoken endings can vary)
- اسمي — is-mee
- شكرا — shuk-ran

Pronunciation guides are approximate. Provide native-speaker audio before relying on transliteration. Do not imply that English letters can represent every Arabic sound exactly.

**Practice**
- Meaning match: audio → English meaning.
- Dialogue order: arrange the four turns.
- Supported recall: choose the missing phrase.
- Optional “hide transliteration” challenge.

**Exit check**
- Identify the meaning of at least 4 of 5 target expressions.
- Correctly identify which chunk asks for a name and which gives a name.

### Session 3 — Have your first conversation (8–12 minutes)

**Mission:** Complete a first-meeting exchange using the learner's own name.

**Scene:** A simple virtual social space with one character approaching the learner. Provide accessible 2D dialogue cards as a fallback.

**Practice ladder**
1. **Echo:** Listen and repeat each line; repetition is optional.
2. **Guided response:** The character says مرحبا; learner chooses أهلا.
3. **Personalized response:** Character asks ما اسمك؟; learner types their name after اسمي.
4. **Reduced support:** Hide English meanings but keep the Arabic phrase cards.
5. **Independent attempt:** Learner completes the exchange in the correct order.
6. **Polite close:** Offer شكرا as a final expression when appropriate.

**Final role-play**
- Agent: مرحبا
- Learner: أهلا
- Agent: ما اسمك؟
- Learner: اسمي [learner's name]
- Agent: تشرفت بلقائك (optional extension only; not a required mastery target)
- Learner: شكرا (acceptable polite response in this simplified scenario)

Keep the optional extension out of the core mastery score because تشرفت بلقائك has not been part of the required five-expression set.

**Final success criteria**
- The learner completes the four required turns in the correct communicative order.
- The learner supplies their own name.
- The learner correctly interprets at least 4 of 5 target expressions.
- The learner identifies at least 3 of 4 target letters.
- A learner may pass the interaction using tap-to-select or text input; microphone access is never mandatory.

## 5. AI tutor behavior — bounded and testable

The first release can use a deterministic/mock tutor. No paid model or external speech API is required for this unit.

**Allowed tutor actions**
- Explain one of the approved five expressions in simple English.
- Give one example using the learner's chosen name.
- Offer a hint after an incorrect answer.
- Generate a new practice prompt only from the approved phrase set and target letter set.
- Ask the learner to retry with less support.
- Summarize demonstrated outcomes using actual recorded attempts.

**Not allowed**
- Invent grammar rules or claim dialect forms are universal.
- Introduce unapproved vocabulary during a scored task.
- Claim the learner has mastered pronunciation based only on speech recognition.
- Claim an answer was spoken when the learner used text input.
- Infer age, nationality, religion, or other personal traits.
- Save voice recordings without explicit, separate consent.

**Example tutor prompts**
- “You heard مرحبا. Does it mean hello, thank you, or goodbye?”
- “The question ما اسمك؟ asks for someone's name. What could you answer?”
- “Try using اسمي followed by your own name.”

## 6. Data and progress events

Record only what is necessary for learning evidence:
- unit and session identifiers;
- activity identifier and attempt number;
- selected answer or submitted text where appropriate;
- correctness and feedback code;
- completion timestamp;
- support level used (English shown, transliteration shown, hint used);
- skill evidence category (listening, interaction, reading, writing).

Do not store microphone audio for this unit. If browser speech recognition is used in a later optional experiment, disclose its limitations and obtain any required permission before microphone use.

## 7. Mobile and accessibility requirements

- Design for a narrow Android viewport first; no horizontal scrolling.
- Large touch targets and clear Arabic glyph rendering.
- Correct RTL rendering for Arabic text, with English explanation remaining LTR.
- Audio controls must have visible labels and replay affordances.
- Every audio activity must have a text equivalent.
- Every role-play must have a no-microphone path.
- Respect reduced-motion settings; animations must not carry essential information alone.
- Provide a 2D fallback if 3D is slow or unavailable.
- Preserve progress after sign-in and verify it can be restored after reload / a new session before calling it persistent.

## 8. Content and assessment QA checklist

Before marking this unit ready for learners:
- [ ] Arabic reviewed by a qualified Arabic-language reviewer.
- [ ] English explanations reviewed for beginner clarity.
- [ ] Native-speaker audio recorded or approved, with attribution and usage rights recorded.
- [ ] Audio speed and replay work on Android Chrome and supported native app surfaces.
- [ ] Every correct and incorrect answer path is tested.
- [ ] Transliteration is labelled approximate and can be hidden.
- [ ] The learner is never required to use a microphone.
- [ ] Role-play can be completed by touch/text.
- [ ] Progress is saved and restored for an authenticated account.
- [ ] A small pilot group of absolute beginners can complete the unit and explain the phrases.
- [ ] Any 3D claim accurately describes the implemented technology.

## 9. Pilot evaluation

Run a small formative pilot with 5–8 English-speaking beginners. This is usability feedback, not statistically representative validation.

Observe:
- Can learners identify the reading direction without coaching?
- Can they distinguish ب، ت، ث by dot placement?
- Can they explain the difference between ما اسمك؟ and اسمي ...?
- Can they complete the four-turn exchange after the three sessions?
- Which transliteration supports are useful, and when do learners choose to hide them?
- Where do learners hesitate, guess, or confuse listening with reading?

**Revision rule:** revise the activity if multiple pilot learners make the same avoidable error or cannot explain the task. Do not lower the learning outcome simply to improve completion metrics.

## 10. Release decision

This document defines the teaching experience and quality gates. It does not claim that all of these activities, audio assets, AI tutor functions, or 3D scenes are already implemented.

Implementation should proceed in small increments:
1. Map the three sessions to the existing lesson content schema.
2. Add only content types already supported by the app, or extend the schema with tests.
3. Build the first session and verify learner progress end-to-end.
4. Add the dialogue and personalized role-play.
5. Add the bounded mock tutor only after the base lesson is reliable.
6. Run the pilot and Arabic-language review before declaring the unit ready.

**Definition of done:** a true beginner can complete the three sessions on an Android phone, understand and use the first-meeting exchange with their own name, demonstrate the specified letter recognition, and see accurate saved progress—without paid AI services or mandatory microphone access.
