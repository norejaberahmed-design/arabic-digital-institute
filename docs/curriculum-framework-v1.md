# Arabic Digital Institute — Curriculum Framework v1

**Audience:** English-speaking adult beginners, initially US learners  
**Course target:** Contemporary, broadly understandable Arabic, with Modern Standard Arabic as the shared written and instructional baseline  
**Status:** Original curriculum design specification; not an accredited CEFR or ACTFL course

## 1. Curriculum design decision

We will learn from the *design principles* used in strong English-language programs and language curricula, then create original Arabic learning content for English-speaking learners. We will not translate or reproduce a protected textbook, course, lesson sequence, exercise bank, or proprietary assessment.

The course is designed around what learners can do in real situations—not around memorizing grammar chapters. Each unit connects a practical communication task to vocabulary, pronunciation, grammar, literacy, cultural context, practice, and assessment.

## 2. Reference frameworks

- **CEFR Companion Volume (Council of Europe, 2020):** use its action-oriented approach and illustrative “can-do” descriptors to plan outcomes across Pre-A1, A1, A2, B1, B2, C1, and C2. Include reception, production, interaction, mediation, online interaction, and phonological competence where appropriate.
- **ACTFL Proficiency Guidelines 2024:** use as a complementary lens for real-world ability in listening, speaking, reading, and writing, including functions/tasks, accuracy, context/content, and text type. Use the interpersonal, interpretive, and presentational communication modes when designing tasks.
- These are design references, not a claim of official alignment, endorsement, certification, or permission to reproduce their protected descriptor text. We will write our own measurable Arabic outcomes and assessment rubrics.

Official references:

## 3. Learner profile and Arabic language policy

### Starting learner assumptions
- The learner may know no Arabic and may have little familiarity with a right-to-left writing system.
- English is the support language at the beginning, not the language in which the learner must remain dependent.
- Lessons must work for adults using an Android phone, with short sessions and clear progress.

### Arabic variety policy
- Teach contemporary, broadly understandable Arabic. Use clear Modern Standard Arabic as the shared written baseline.
- Do not force one regional dialect on all learners.
- Where spoken regional alternatives matter, label the region and context explicitly, compare alternatives, and teach the shared form first.
- Explain when a phrase is formal, neutral, conversational, or region-specific.

### Script, vowels, and transliteration
- Introduce right-to-left direction and letter joining explicitly.
- Teach each letter in isolated, initial, medial, and final forms, including letters that do not connect to the following letter.
- Keep ordinary beginner reading mostly unvowelled, as requested for this course.
- Introduce short vowels and other diacritics deliberately as a decoding and contrast tool. Include activities where learners add the vowel marks themselves.
- Provide simple English-letter pronunciation support early, but reduce it in stages. Never make transliteration a permanent substitute for reading Arabic.
- Teach Arabic-Indic and Western digits as recognition variants where useful.

## 4. Course level map

The following is the institute's planned scope. Outcomes are original working targets and must be validated with learner testing before being described as formal proficiency equivalence.

| Stage | Main communicative outcome | Language and literacy focus | Evidence of learning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pre-A1 / Starter | Recognize Arabic sounds and script; exchange a few memorized greetings and personal details | Directionality, letter shapes and connections, high-frequency sounds, basic words, simple phrases | Identify and write target letters; recognize familiar words; complete a supported greeting exchange |
| A1 | Handle simple everyday exchanges when the other person speaks clearly and helps | Personal information, family, numbers, time, places, requests; basic nominal/verbal patterns | Ask and answer familiar questions; understand short supported messages; write simple personal sentences |
| A2 | Manage predictable routine interactions | Shopping, food, directions, travel, daily routine, past/future time references; common agreement and verb patterns | Complete short transactions; describe routine and past events in connected simple sentences |
| B1 | Deal with familiar situations independently and explain experiences and plans | Work, study, health, services, travel problems, opinions; paragraph structure and connected discourse | Sustain a practical conversation; summarize a short text; write connected paragraphs |
| B2 | Participate with growing fluency in a wider range of concrete and abstract topics | Nuance, register, argument, media, workplace communication, complex sentence patterns | Support an opinion with reasons; follow extended speech; produce organized explanations and reports |
| C1 | Use Arabic flexibly and effectively in academic, professional, and social contexts | Style, cohesion, idiom, register shifts, dense texts, implicit meaning | Understand demanding texts and extended speech; communicate fluently with well-controlled structure |
| C2 | Interpret and express highly nuanced meaning across complex contexts | Fine semantic distinctions, rhetoric, style, cultural references, advanced register | Demonstrate precise comprehension and expression across unfamiliar and complex tasks |

Each level will be divided into thematic units. The progression is spiral: important vocabulary, sounds, structures, and functions return in richer contexts rather than appearing once and disappearing.

## 5. Pre-A1 and A1 first-release scope

### Pre-A1: Arabic foundations
1. Welcome to Arabic: goals, sound, script direction, and how to use the learning space.
2. Letter shapes I: ا ب ت ث — recognition, sound, tracing, and shape positions.
3. Letter shapes II: ج ح خ د ذ — contrast and articulation awareness.
4. Letter shapes III: ر ز س ش — recognition and reading practice.
5. Letter shapes IV: ص ض ط ظ — emphatic-sound awareness and visual discrimination.
6. Letter shapes V: ع غ ف ق ك — guided listening and production.
7. Letter shapes VI: ل م ن هـ و ي — recognition and connected forms.
8. Joining letters: build and decode short words; learn non-connecting letters.
9. Short vowels: identify and add fatHa, kasra, and damma to selected examples.
10. First words: باب، بيت، بنت and other controlled words; decode without permanent transliteration.
11. Greetings and polite expressions: مرحبا، أهلا، شكرا، من فضلك.
12. First exchange: say a name, ask a name, and respond politely.

### A1: Everyday communication
- Unit 1: Introductions and personal information.
- Unit 2: Countries, languages, and where people are from.
- Unit 3: Family and describing people.
- Unit 4: Numbers, dates, clock time, and simple schedules.
- Unit 5: Food, cafés, and polite requests.
- Unit 6: Places, directions, and transport.
- Unit 7: Daily routines and common actions.
- Unit 8: Home, objects, and simple descriptions.
- Unit 9: Shopping, quantities, and prices.
- Unit 10: Review project—complete a short day-in-Arabic sequence.

Existing seeded lessons are a starter content set, not proof that the full Pre-A1/A1 curriculum is complete. Before publishing each lesson as a finished course unit, it must pass the lesson-quality checklist in section 7.

## 6. Learning architecture

Every unit follows a consistent cycle:

1. **Real-life mission:** show a concrete situation and a “can do” goal.
2. **Notice:** introduce a short model dialogue, audio, or visual example.
3. **Understand:** check meaning before asking learners to produce language.
4. **Build:** teach a small set of useful words and one focused language pattern.
5. **Hear:** use clear native-speaker audio with normal and slowed playback where available.
6. **Say:** guided repetition, then meaningful substitution and a short response.
7. **Read:** decode authentic-looking but level-controlled Arabic text.
8. **Write:** trace or assemble letters at the earliest stage, then complete and create messages.
9. **Interact:** use a role-play or branching scenario in the virtual institute.
10. **Retrieve:** recall earlier language without showing the answer first.
11. **Check:** short formative assessment and actionable feedback.
12. **Reflect:** learner self-check against the lesson goal and next-step recommendation.

Lessons should normally be broken into mobile-friendly 5–12 minute activities. A complete unit can contain several short sessions rather than forcing a long sitting.

## 7. Required lesson specification

No lesson is considered ready until it contains:

- Level, unit, lesson ID, prerequisites, and estimated time.
- One primary measurable communication outcome and up to three supporting outcomes.
- Separate listening, speaking, reading, and writing objectives where relevant.
- Target vocabulary with Arabic, meaning, audio reference, and transliteration only when pedagogically justified.
- A short model dialogue or contextual text written originally for the course.
- One focused grammar or language-pattern explanation, not a disconnected grammar dump.
- A pronunciation focus, including likely English-speaker difficulty where relevant.
- A literacy focus when the lesson includes Arabic script.
- At least one interpretive task, one productive task, and one interpersonal task across the lesson or its unit.
- Practice that moves from recognition to supported recall to independent use.
- Immediate feedback explaining why an answer works.
- A review item that retrieves previously learned language.
- A final “can do” check with a transparent success criterion.
- Audio attribution and content provenance recorded before publication.

### Mastery rule
A quiz score alone does not establish communicative mastery. A unit completion decision should combine:
- comprehension evidence;
- learner production or interaction evidence;
- literacy evidence when applicable;
- delayed retrieval/review evidence.

Working product rule: target at least 80% on objective recognition checks, plus successful completion of the unit's communication task. If the learner misses the task, provide targeted practice and another attempt rather than simply blocking progress.

## 8. Assessment and progress model

- **Placement:** a low-pressure diagnostic that profiles script familiarity and the four skills separately. Do not assume equal skill levels.
- **Formative checks:** short checks during lessons to detect misunderstandings early.
- **Unit assessment:** contextual task plus objective checks.
- **Cumulative review:** spaced retrieval across earlier units, with increasing intervals.
- **Skill profile:** report listening, speaking, reading, and writing separately; do not collapse them into one unexplained score.
- **Can-do portfolio:** save completed tasks and learner reflections as evidence of progress.
- **Rubrics:** describe comprehensibility, task completion, vocabulary range, control, fluency, and text organization at a level-appropriate scale.

### Speaking assessment honesty
The current browser speech-recognition feature can compare a recognized transcript with a target phrase when supported by the browser. It is not a validated phonetic assessment and cannot reliably score articulation, accent, or exact pronunciation quality. The interface must say so. Genuine pronunciation feedback requires suitable recorded native-speaker models and a separately validated speech-assessment method; do not claim this capability until implemented and tested.

## 9. English scaffolding policy

- Pre-A1: clear English instructions, concise meaning support, simple pronunciation guide, and visual examples.
- A1: English explanations remain available, but prompts increasingly invite Arabic responses.
- A2: reduce word-for-word translation; use Arabic context, images, and short Arabic instructions with optional English help.
- B1: default to Arabic for task instructions and explanations; English is an optional support layer.
- B2–C2: Arabic-first learning, with English available only for selected complex comparisons or accessibility needs.

The learner must always be able to request help. Reducing support is gradual and responsive, not a punishment or an abrupt language switch.

## 10. 3D learning experience requirements

3D must teach, not merely decorate.

- **Campus:** orientation, progress, level selection, and learner goals.
- **Classroom:** teacher presence, board, objects, and guided lesson activities.
- **Letter studio:** rotate or inspect letter forms; compare isolated/initial/medial/final shapes; assemble letters into words.
- **Sound studio:** hear minimal contrasts and connect sound, mouth-position explanation, and written form.
- **Role-play spaces:** café, home, shop, transport station, workplace, and public service scenarios.
- **Games:** purposeful retrieval, listening discrimination, word building, sentence ordering, and dialogue choices.
- **Accessibility:** captions, keyboard/touch alternatives, reduced motion, readable contrast, and non-3D fallback.
- **Android performance:** load only the active scene, use lightweight assets, test on mid-range phones, and preserve a fast 2D path when 3D is unsupported.

The current homepage is a first CSS-based 3D-style milestone. It is not yet a complete WebGL 3D institute, and the existing lesson routes are not yet full 3D classrooms.

## 11. Cultural and linguistic quality controls

- Use respectful, diverse, everyday contexts without presenting one culture or country as the only Arabic-speaking experience.
- Explain social conventions such as greetings, politeness, names, and forms of address with context.
- Avoid teaching region-specific expressions as universal Arabic.
- Review grammar, spelling, translation, transliteration, and audio with qualified Arabic-language reviewers before public release.
- Label uncertain or variable usage honestly; do not invent linguistic rules.
- Do not use machine-generated audio as the only pronunciation model for critical sound contrasts.

## 12. Release gates

A level or unit can be marked **Ready for learners** only after:
1. Outcomes are measurable and mapped to the level plan.
2. Content is original and its source/licensing status is documented.
3. Arabic language review and English editorial review are complete.
4. Audio is checked for clarity, correctness, and speaker attribution.
5. Activities work on a phone and with touch input.
6. Correct and incorrect answers are tested.
7. Progress is saved and restored correctly for an authenticated learner.
8. Accessibility and reduced-motion alternatives are checked.
9. A pilot learner can complete the unit and explain what they learned.
10. No marketing copy implies official CEFR/ACTFL certification or a speech-scoring capability we do not have.
